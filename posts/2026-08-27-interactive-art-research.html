Finding Interactive Art: Research & Reflection

For this assignment I went looking for a piece of interactive art worth sitting with for a while. I landed on "The Pool" by Jen Lewin, a traveling light-and-sound installation I came across while reading through a roundup of standout interactive art examples online.

Who made it, and how

Jen Lewin, a Brooklyn based sculptor and engineer with an architectural background first built The Pool for Burning Man in 2008, and has continued touring and refining it ever since. The piece is made up of 106 circular pads arranged in concentric rings that make more of a giant painted target than a swimming pool. Each pad has its own wireless controller and lights up when stepped on, and there's no central computer or router coordinating the whole thing. Instead, every pad runs on the same simple rule set and reacts to its neighbors in real time so light and color ripple outward the same way a footstep sends ripples across a pond. Walk, jump, or dance across the pads and you're effectively playing a giant game of light ping pong with everyone else standing on it.

Who it's for

The Pool is built for a crowd and not a solo viewer. It shows up at festivals, plazas, and public squares (ex: Burning Man, Vivid Sydney, the Hong Kong Arts Festival), and dozens of other cities and events across sixty plus exhibits in the last thirteen years. It's designed so that anyone walking by whether a kid or adult, can step on and immediately understand what to do without instructions. There's no instruction, no screen, and no explanation needed, the invitation is just the light itself. The goal isn't to have people admire the work from a distance, it's to draw them in as collaborators, so the "audience" is really whoever happens to be nearby and willing to play. This also allows the audience to not just be the audience, but also the creator.

What it's like to experience

When stepped on, the pads have a little give, like a padded gym floor, and each step triggers a small burst of color that spreads outward beneath your feet before fading. At night the whole installation glows against the dark, and a crowd moving across it looks like a field of water lit from below. There's a rhythmic thump of dozens of people jumping and landing, possibly occasional laughter or joy when someone's footstep sets off a chain reaction across the rings. The emotional arc is quick with an immediate delight at the visual payoff, and then something more collaborative. One may start timing their steps with strangers, testing what happens if you both land at once, watching how your pattern blends into someone else's. It's less like viewing art and more like being handed an instrument you already know how to play.

Lewin describes The Pool as "a giant canvas where you can paint and splash light collaboratively" — a description that lines up with how it actually feels to stand on it. (Salience)
